The Ten Plagues

Ten signs upon a hardened heart

Rameses (Land of Goshen) · c. 1446 BC · Ex 7:14–11:10

Story

Moses raised his staff and struck the Nile, and the river turned to blood. Dead fish floated to the surface, and the stench was so great that the Egyptians could not drink from the river. Seven days later frogs crawled up out of the river and covered everything, even the bedrooms of the palace, the ovens and the kneading bowls. The dust of the ground became gnats swarming over people and animals. Here the magicians, who had copied the signs, gave up and said to Pharaoh, this is the finger of God.

Next came swarms of flies, filling the palace and every house. But in the land of Goshen, where the Israelites lived, there was not a single one. The livestock sickened and fell, and boils broke out on people and animals. Thunder rolled across the sky, and hail mixed with fire beat down on the fields. Then locusts, carried in on the east wind, devoured every green thing the hail had spared. After that, for three days, a darkness so thick it could be felt covered the land.

Even in that darkness there was light in the places where the Israelites lived. Whenever a plague grew too hard to bear, Pharaoh gave in, again and again. But each time it stopped, he hardened his heart again. At last Moses announced the final plague. At midnight every firstborn in Egypt would die, from the firstborn son of Pharaoh on his throne to the firstborn son of the slave girl at her millstone, down to the firstborn of the livestock. A heavy silence settled over the land.

The sayings are fresh renderings of the original meaning in today's language. Text: Ex 7:14–11:10

Background

The plagues are sometimes read as judgments on the gods Egypt worshipped, such as the Nile and the sun. The Lord himself said he would bring judgment on all the gods of Egypt. Some try to explain the plagues as a chain of natural events, but views differ.
